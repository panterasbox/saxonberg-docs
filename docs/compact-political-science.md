# The Compact as political science

**Status: lore-adjacent + course constraint.** This document authors
the **course treatment** of the Compact — what real political science
it teaches, what is invented and what is not, how it is assessed, and
what a student actually *does*. It is the social-science sibling of
[arcane-science.md](./arcane-science.md), and it stands in the same
relation to its subject: it changes no mechanic, it constrains what
may be taught, and it exists so a course can be synthesized from it.

The difference from its sibling is that here **the subject already
exists.** Thaumology had to be invented; the Compact was designed, and
its primary sources are on disk:

- [governance/draft-constitution.md](./governance/draft-constitution.md)
  — the articles, the eternity clauses, and the Schedule of
  Parameters.
- [governance/founding-charter.md](./governance/founding-charter.md) —
  the founder's self-binding commitment.
- `slates/builds/cooperative-slate.md` — the rationale behind the
  provisions.
- `polity-decision-register.md` — the decisions and why they went the
  way they did.

What's missing is not the subject. It's the pedagogy.

## The hard rule, in the social-science register

The physics course has one rail: *nothing may contradict real
science.* A political science course cannot have that rail in the same
form, because most of its questions are contested rather than settled,
and a course that resolved them would be indoctrination rather than
instruction. The correct analog is four rails:

1. **Never misrepresent how a real institution works.** Comparative
   claims about real constitutions, courts, legislatures, and firms
   must be accurate and checkable.
2. **Never present a contested normative question as settled.** Where
   reasonable people disagree, the course teaches the disagreement.
3. **The Compact is a case, not a model answer.** Its designers'
   preferences are taught as preferences, and every major design
   decision is presented with the strongest available case against it.
4. **Where the Compact departs from real practice, name what real
   practice does and why.** A departure is interesting *because* of
   the comparison, not instead of it.

### The legitimacy hazard, stated plainly

This course is taught inside the institution it studies, on a platform
its founder built, using that founder's own constitution as the
primary text. That is a real conflict and pretending otherwise would
be the fastest way to discredit the whole education thesis.

The rule that resolves it:

> **A student must be able to earn the top grade in this course by
> arguing, well, that the Compact is badly designed and should not be
> adopted.**

If that is not true, the course is marketing. It should be stated in
the syllabus, not buried here — and the grading rubric must reward the
quality of the argument, never its direction. The set of assigned
comparison cases must likewise be drawn from across the political
spectrum; a reading list that only indicts one direction is a defect
in the course, regardless of anyone's private views.

As with the physics course: **the invented part is the case; the
analytical apparatus a student is graded on is entirely real.**

## Why 200-level

The course presumes working knowledge of:

- how a real state is organized — separation of powers, legislative
  procedure, judicial review, federalism;
- how a real firm is governed — shares, boards, fiduciary duty, the
  principal–agent problem;
- the basic vocabulary of both, well enough to notice when the Compact
  is doing something unusual rather than merely unfamiliar.

Without those, the interesting features read as arbitrary. With them,
almost every article is legible as a deliberate departure from a
known baseline — which is the entire pedagogical value.

## The unusual teaching object

Four properties make this a better object of study than most real
constitutions, and they should be named to students early because
they change how the material can be worked.

**The framing record is complete.** Real constitutional intent is
reconstructed from fragments — the Federalist Papers were advocacy,
Madison's notes were published fifty years later, and most
constitutions have no record at all. Here the design conversation, the
rejected alternatives, and the reasons are all on disk. A student can
ask *why is it this way* and get an actual answer rather than an
inference. That also lets the course teach something real: **how much
weight framers' intent should carry**, tested on a case where intent
is not in doubt.

**Uncertainty is marked.** Five articles carry an explicit **[OPEN]**
flag with a recommendation rather than a provision. A constitution
that admits which parts its authors were unsure about is a rare
document and a superb teaching aid — the open articles are where
student work goes.

**The configuration is separated from the logic.** The Schedule of
Parameters names every tunable value once, with the tier of difficulty
required to change it. That separation — *structure changes by
amendment, constants change by organic law* — is itself a piece of
constitutional design worth a seminar, and it makes comparative
experiments possible.

**It runs.** This is the practicum thesis in the political-science
register. The difference between reading about a legislature and
watching your own bill lapse below the survival floor is the
difference between instruction and application.

### Code as a regulatory modality

Lessig's claim — that code is one of four modalities regulating
behavior, alongside law, norms, and markets — is standard curriculum,
and the Compact takes an unusually precise position within it.

Art. I §7 commits to enforcement by code *where the law is
mechanically applicable*, and then Art. V §9 draws the line that most
treatments of algorithmic governance leave blurry: **code enforces;
administration is human and is never automated.** The polity may shape
administration only by tuning parameters and by oversight, never by
replacing judgment with code.

That is a substantive, arguable position in a live real-world debate
about automated decision-making, and it can be assessed against real
material — the street-level-bureaucracy literature on discretion, the
accountability problems in automated benefits and sentencing systems,
and the general question of what is lost when discretion is removed.
Students should be able to argue both that this line is drawn in the
right place and that it is drawn too generously.

### Exit, and what it does to constitutional theory

The dead-hand problem is old: Jefferson thought each generation should
re-found, Madison thought stability required entrenchment, and every
constitution since has been some compromise. The Compact's answer is
unusual — eternity clauses are genuinely unamendable, and the only
route past them is **to found a new polity and migrate** (Art. X §4).

That converts a constitutional problem into an exit problem, which is
Hirschman's frame, and the Compact makes exit far cheaper than any
real polity can: you leave with your data and your creations. Tiebout
sorting, normally a theory about municipalities, becomes something
close to literal.

The seminar question is genuinely open, and students should argue it
rather than be told: **when exit is cheap, does voice get stronger or
weaker?** The optimistic reading is that credible exit disciplines an
operator better than any right. The pessimistic reading is that cheap
exit lets dissenters leave instead of fixing things, so communities
fragment into like-minded shards and the capacity for self-government
atrophies — the case against is at least as strong as the case for.

### The three chambers

This is the Compact's most distinctive invention, and it is easy to
teach wrongly. The chambers are **not** three groups of people. Each
member holds all three kinds of influence, earns each by the matching
kind of contribution, and allocates each independently. A bill is
tallied three ways across the *same* crowd and carries on two of three.

The real comparison set is functional and corporatist representation —
vocational panels, works councils, tripartite social dialogue,
multi-stakeholder cooperatives — and the standard objections to those
apply here and should be taught: functional constituencies are
notoriously gameable, boundary definitions become political, and
"which chamber does this contribution count in" is a question with no
neutral answer.

The specific consequence worth working through carefully: **a large
funder dominates the capital tally and still cannot carry a bill
alone.** Whether that is a genuine constraint on money or a
laundering of it into legitimacy is exactly the kind of question a
200-level course should leave students arguing about.

### Sortition

Art. VI's jury pool scales from a pool of one — an operator hearing
their own appeals — to a sortition of the citizenry drawn by lot, with
influence and reputation counting for nothing once the pool is the
citizenry.

Sortition is having a genuine revival in real democratic theory and
practice: Athenian precedent, Ireland's citizens' assemblies feeding
constitutional referendums, France's climate convention, and — the
most institutionally advanced case — **Ostbelgien**, where since 2019
Belgium's German-speaking Community has run a *permanent* Citizens'
Council of 24 members serving 18-month terms, which convenes Citizens'
Assemblies alongside the elected parliament. The literature on when
sortition outperforms election, and on its known weaknesses — no
accountability to constituents, no mandate, vulnerability to
facilitation effects — is real, current, and directly applicable.

**Ostbelgien also supplies the course's sharpest concrete comparison,
on a problem both systems have to solve: how do you know the lottery
was fair?** Ostbelgien's answer is institutional — the draw is
overseen by a judge. The Compact's answer is constitutional and
cryptographic: Art. VII §2 requires that the random draws, like every
other integrity output, be **independently re-derivable by any
member**, so that *no party need be trusted.*

Two solutions to one problem — **trust a trusted person, or remove the
need for trust by construction** — and the tradeoff is real in both
directions. A judge is legible to anyone, needs no technical literacy
to accept, and carries the weight of an office people already
understand. A verifiable draw needs no honest official, and also needs
a citizenry able and willing to check it, or it is merely a different
kind of faith. That is a genuinely undecided question in real
institutional design, and it is the right shape for a seminar: not
"which is better" but "under what conditions does each fail?"

The Compact adds something the real cases mostly lack: the same
process runs at every scale, so a community that already hears appeals
adopts no new procedure when it ratifies, only wider settings. That's
a design idea worth examining on its own — **institutional continuity
across scale** — and it is unusual enough to be worth a paper.

## The design decisions worth arguing

These are the seminar spine. Each is a real choice with a real cost,
and the course's credibility depends on the case against being put as
well as the case for.

**Weighted governance at all.** The Compact weights votes by
contribution, which is a deliberate departure from one-person-one-vote
— itself hard-won and defended for good reasons. The case for:
contribution-weighting matches voice to stake and to knowledge. The
case against: every historical franchise restricted by property,
literacy, or contribution was defended in exactly those terms, and
they were wrong. Students should read the historical defenses of
restricted franchise before deciding.

**Money as an open chamber.** The Compact's position is that money's
influence in real politics is unbounded and hidden, so bounding it and
making it visible is strictly better. The case against: legitimating
paid influence normalizes it, and a bounded formal channel may simply
run alongside the informal one rather than replacing it.

**Eternity clauses.** Real precedent exists — the German Basic Law's
unamendable core, Brazil's stone clauses, Italy's republican form. The
case against is the standard one: unamendability is a bet by the
founding generation that it knew better than every generation after
it, and the Compact's bet includes economic provisions, which is a
stronger claim than protecting human dignity.

**Enforcement by code.** Uniform, unbribable, unbiased by mood — and
also inflexible, opaque to those it governs, and unable to recognize
the case the rule did not anticipate. Mercy is discretion. A system
that cannot exercise discretion cannot be merciful.

**The founder's fiat phase.** The charter is a genuine precommitment
device: formula-fixed, code-enforced, published, auto-sunsetting. The
case against: a self-imposed constraint is not law, its enforcement
depends on the constrained party running the code, and "I have bound
myself" is what every founder says. Michels' iron law of oligarchy is
the assigned counterweight.

**Requirements-not-implementation.** The legislature says what the
rule is; the executive builds it. Clean in principle, and it relocates
enormous power to whoever writes the code — which is the real
delegation problem in a new costume. Whether judicial verification is
a sufficient check is an empirical question nobody has answered.

## The practicum

What a student actually does, rather than reads about. This is the
half study.com's category structurally cannot supply.

- **Hold influence in three chambers and allocate it.** Conviction
  weighting means you feel the build and the decay; moving an
  allocation costs you something legible.
- **Table a bill and watch it lapse** below the survival floor. Learn
  abandonment dynamics by losing.
- **Serve on a jury** when you are drawn by lot, and render a verdict
  under the fixed rules of sound reasoning — completeness,
  consistency, grounding, conformance to law, freedom from forbidden
  basis.
- **Watch a house drop below quorum and abstain**, and discover
  first-hand that the remaining two must both assent.
- **Audit the archive.** The record is tamper-evident and every
  integrity output is independently re-derivable, so a student can
  verify a tally themselves rather than trusting it. That is a real
  and increasingly employable skill — election auditing and
  risk-limiting audits are the same competence.
- **Draft an open article.** Five are genuinely unresolved.

### Controlled experiments in institutional design

This deserves its own note, because it is the closest thing to a
calorimeter this course has.

Real comparative politics is observational. You cannot re-run France
with a different electoral system, so causal claims about
institutions are inferred from messy natural variation. Here the
Schedule of Parameters is explicitly tunable, which means a course
can **run the same polity twice with a different passage threshold**
and observe what changes.

**The honest caveats are the lesson.** The population is small, so
experiments are underpowered. Participants know they are in an
experiment. The world is not a state, and nothing here transfers to
real institutions without a serious external-validity argument. A
student who runs a parameter experiment and then writes carefully
about why its results do *not* generalize has learned more
methodology than one who runs a clean regression on someone else's
dataset.

## Assessment

The interesting structural result, and worth teaching explicitly
because it is a methodological lesson in its own right:

> **The line between machine-gradable and human-gradable falls exactly
> on the positive/normative line.**

In thaumology almost everything is positive, so almost everything is
machine-gradable. Here the course is split down the middle, and the
assessment architecture makes the split visible.

**Machine-gradable — the constitution is a deterministic evaluator.**
The procedural item generator that serves Magic 101 works here too;
it just runs over rules instead of physics.

- *Given this allocation history, these parameters, and this
  membership, does the bill carry?* The tally is deterministic.
- *House C has fallen below quorum. Given the other two houses'
  weights, does the bill become law?* Deterministic, and it tests the
  abstention rule that students most often get wrong.
- *Which amendment tier governs this change?* Deterministic from
  Art. X plus the Schedule.
- *Is this executive act justiciable, and under which article?*
- *Here is an archive excerpt. Verify the count.*

Every one of those has a computed key, generates unbounded variants,
and cannot be answer-shared. The requirement the generator has is a
**deterministic evaluator**, and a well-specified constitution is one
— which is a nice incidental argument for specifying constitutions
well.

**Human-gradable — and correctly so.** *Was this act fair? Should this
bill have passed? Is the founder's charter a real constraint?* These
have no key, they should not have one, and grading them by rubric on
argument quality is exactly what a real political science course does.
The essay and the oral defense carry this half.

**Deed.** The engine witnessed you allocate, sponsor, serve, and
verify. Participation evidence without self-report.

## Misconceptions

The teaching bank, and — as in the sibling course — a seed bank for
plausible-but-wrong student and NPC positions.

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| "The three chambers are three groups of people." | They are three measures of every member. One crowd, three tallies. |
| "Capital influence is equity." | Stake is not stock. No dividend, no cash-out, no redeemable claim. |
| "Money can buy a decision." | It carries one house of three. Two are needed. |
| "Influence is a holding you own." | It is a reservoir that regenerates with contribution and decays when moved. |
| "Absence is punished." | Absence stops the faucet; it never confiscates the reservoir. |
| "A house that goes quiet blocks everything." | Below quorum it abstains — and passage is still measured against the full count, so the others must carry it. |
| "The judiciary makes policy." | It judges verification and spirit, and voids acts. Policy is legislative. |
| "Ratifying rights builds new machinery." | The machinery already exists. Ratification removes operator discretion over it. |
| "Eternity clauses are undemocratic in a way real constitutions aren't." | Several real constitutions have them. The argument is about scope, not existence. |
| "The founder's charter is a legal guarantee." | It is a precommitment enforced by code, publication, and exit — explicitly not law. |
| "Code enforcement means the polity is run by code." | Code enforces. Administration is human by constitutional requirement. |

## Open problems

Unlike the physics course, these are open *in the document itself* —
five articles carry an explicit **[OPEN]** flag. Student work goes
here, and good student work could in principle become real proposals,
which is a strong motivator and should be honestly caveated (nothing
obligates the polity to adopt anything).

1. **Membership and the franchise (Art. II).** What participation
   threshold converts a participant into a citizen? Every answer
   excludes someone.
2. **Rights (Art. IX).** Deliberately left to a ratifying convention
   rather than dictated by the founder. Drafting a bill of rights for
   a digital polity is the course's natural capstone.
3. **Founding and transition (Art. XI).** What population threshold
   should trigger ratification, and what makes it legitimate?
4. **Emergency powers (Art. XII).** The oldest problem in
   constitutional design, with the standard failure mode well
   documented.
5. **Interpretation (Art. XIII).** Who reads the constitution, and how
   much does a complete framing record change the answer?

Two more that the documents raise without resolving: the precise
reconciliation of a capped, regenerating reservoir with continuous
allocation (Art. IV §2 says outright that it *remains to be
specified*), and the whole question of whether contribution-weighted
governance is defensible at all.

## Course-readiness

As with the sibling document, the syllabus is not this document's job.
What it guarantees is that the material exists in the shape a course
needs, across the strands a real political science course carries:

- **Institutional analysis** — separation of powers, delegation,
  bicameralism, entrenchment, quorum and collapse dynamics.
- **Theory** — social choice, precommitment, exit/voice/loyalty,
  commons governance, code as a regulatory modality.
- **Comparative method** — real constitutions and real firms as the
  baseline the Compact departs from, drawn from across the spectrum.
- **Primary-source reading** — a constitution, a charter, and a
  complete framing record, read the way a law student reads:
  provision, purpose, tension, silence.
- **Quantitative** — deterministic tallies, threshold and quorum
  arithmetic, the parameter schedule, and the honest limits of
  small-n institutional experiment.
- **Writing and argument** — the graded half is normative, defended in
  essay and oral defense, judged on quality and never on direction.
- **Practicum** — allocate, sponsor, serve, audit, draft.

Two invented subjects, one natural and one social, demonstrating that
the method generalizes across a catalog rather than that magic was a
special case. That generalization is the actual claim; this document
is its second leg.
